Specification Games: Witness-Based Specification Strength
and the Vacuity Gate as a Reward

Vladimir Stoliarov

October 29, 2026

Abstract

This note studies the vacuity gate of Trusted Code Generation (TCG), which accepts a specification
only if it rejects declared faulty variants of the artifact, read as an adversary’s move against a
specification-writing generator that is paid by a kernel verdict. We argue that the gate has precise
mathematical content and that this content yields falsifiable hypotheses about LLM pipelines that
write their own specifications. The mutation score, adequacy and the equivalent-mutant problem
are standard in mutation analysis; program mutation as a completeness measure for specifications,
and the loop in which an LLM refines a specification against a verifier and that score, have been
published. What is claimed here is a certified form of the kill-set order on specifications, computed
from kernel-checked witnesses; a sequential adversarial evaluation whose payoff is the TCG verdict;
four short statements, two about its optima and two about what a certified score guarantees; and
hypotheses about what an optimizer does to the metric under pressure. What is not claimed is
that LLMs behave as the optimizers in these statements, that any mutant library is complete for a
vulnerability class, or that the results transfer beyond executable semantics. This is a research note
with an experimental program, not a results paper.

3 Setting

We work inside one verifiable domain D of v3 [2] (Definition 7), with two additional conditions that
make witnesses kernel-checkable. Here “kernel” means the domain’s trusted certificate checker: a
proof-assistant kernel such as Lean’s, or, in an SMT-backed instantiation, the checker that replays a
counterexample as a ground execution (Section 10).

From v2/v3 we inherit: artifacts b ∈ B; configurations Σ; a specification ϕ = (Pre, Trace, Post, Exit)
over configurations and finite traces; the semantic claim b |= ϕ (every execution of b from a configuration
satisfying Pre has a trace satisfying Trace, ends in a configuration satisfying Post, with exit status
satisfying Exit); the checker Check(b, ϕ, c) (Check(ϕ, b, a, c) of v2, with the annotations a folded into c)
with results Accept, Refute(w), Unknown; and Assumption 3 of v2 [1] (soundness): Check(b, ϕ, c) =
Accept implies b |= ϕ. From v3 we also take the trust classes T (trusted-formal), T ∗ (trusted non-formal,
borne by the human oracle H) and U (untrusted), and the hard, evidence and provenance layers of the
bundle with its session ledger (v3, Sections 12, 13 and 17). Observational equivalence b ≡ b′ means b
and b′ produce the same observable traces from every configuration; by construction ϕ is stated over
observables, so b ≡ b′ implies (b |= ϕ ⇐⇒ b′ |= ϕ).

Condition E (executable, deterministic, observable semantics). There is a computable function run : B ×
Σ → Traces such that run(b, σ ) is the finite, resource-bounded observable trace of b from σ . Determinism
is assumed: the environment, the caller, and any scheduler are part of σ , so one configuration fixes one
execution; a domain with residual nondeterminism needs a set-valued run and witnesses quantified over
that set, which is outside this note. Resource exhaustion (out of gas) is an exit status and is handled by the
Exit component of ϕ, not by exclusion. Traces are observable by construction: ≡ compares run(b, σ ) and
run(b′, σ ), so a single configuration on which they differ is a proof of ̸≡. For EVM this is gas-bounded
evaluation with the block and call context in σ ; in Lean it is a definition the kernel reduces.

Condition D (decidable components). Pre is decidable on configurations; Trace, Post and Exit are
decidable on finite traces, final configurations and exit statuses respectively. Then “run(b, σ ) violates ϕ”
is a ground proposition the kernel decides by evaluation.

These two conditions are what the gate needs beyond a verifiable domain in the sense of v3 [2]
(Definition 7, Section 11). Everything below holds for any domain satisfying them; nothing below uses
EVM except that E and D are cheap there.

4 Mutation classes

Sections 4–6 are background: standard mutation analysis transported to specifications, with one change of
judge (witnesses checked by the kernel instead of a tool report). A mutation class fixes what the adversary
is allowed to do; its V -completeness (Definition 4.3) is the assumption, beyond checker soundness, on
which the security content of Section 8 rests.

Definition 4.1 (mutation class). A mutation class at b is a finite, nonempty set M of artifacts b′ ∈ B, each
identified by id(b′), together with provenance metadata: the operator that produced it and the fault class it
is meant to represent. Identity is by hash, so what a witness refers to is fixed without re-applying any
operator (Section 10). Two mutants with equal hashes are one element; two with different hashes but
equal behavior are counted twice, a bias recorded in Section 11.

3

Definition 4.2 (properness). M is proper at b if for every b′ ∈ M there is a configuration σ with
run(b, σ ) ̸= run(b′, σ ). Since traces are observable (Condition E), this is b′ ̸≡ b. Properness evidence is
the pair (b′, σ ), and the inequality is a ground fact the kernel evaluates.

Properness is where the classical equivalent-mutant problem lives: deciding b′ ≡ b is undecidable in
general. We do not decide it. We construct M by injecting named faults (remove a balance check, reorder
effects and interactions, drop an access control, shift a bound by one), and each injection comes with the
σ on which the mutant visibly differs. Two things are then distinct in the bundle: the inequivalence at σ ,
which the kernel checks and which is therefore T ; and the claim that the mutant represents the named
fault, which is a label and stays T ∗. A mutant that does not compile or deploy is not an artifact and is not
in M; it is never counted as killed. An artifact that always reverts is proper and stays in M.

Definition 4.3 (vulnerability class and completeness). A vulnerability class is a set V ⊆ B closed under
≡. M is V -complete on a set N ⊆ B at b if for every b′′ ∈ N ∩V there is b′ ∈ M with b′′ ≡ b′. N is the
neighborhood of interest, for instance the artifacts a generator could produce from the same intent.

V -completeness is a strong assumption: it demands whole-program observational equivalence between
every V -vulnerable variant in N and some declared mutant, not membership in a fault family. Two
independent reentrancy faults in different functions are not equivalent, so a library with one of them is not
complete for reentrancy on any N that contains the other. V -completeness cannot in general be proved
inside the framework; it is a signed assumption whose scope is exactly the N it names, and Section 8
states what follows when it holds and nothing when it does not.

5 Witnesses and killing

A witness turns “this mutant is caught” from a claim into a kernel-checked fact; the note keeps two
kill sets apart, the semantic one, fixed by the semantics, and the certified one, established by presented
witnesses, because only the second is checkable.

Definition 5.1 (witness). A witness against (b, ϕ) is a configuration σ with Pre(σ ) such that run(b, σ )
violates ϕ. Under Conditions E and D the proposition “σ is a witness against (b, ϕ)” is ground and
decidable, so a witness is a certificate the kernel checks by evaluation. In the SMT instantiation of v2 this
is the counterexample carried by Refute(w); in Lean it is a proof term closed by decide or rfl.

Accept for every c.

Proof. The first part is the definition of |=; the second is Assumption 3 of v2.

Certified adequacy is the only form the bundle can carry, and it includes Accept on b on purpose: an
M-adequate ϕ with Pre = ⊤ and Post = ⊥ rejects every mutant and also rejects b, so adequacy alone
measures discriminating power, not correctness of b or fidelity to intent.

Two remarks fix the meaning of these definitions.
Killing is semantic; certifying is not. Ksem depends on run, M, b and ϕ only, never on which prover
or adversary is used; that is the gate’s analogue of capability-invariance (Proposition 1 of v2). But what
can be checked for finite M is exactly: positive adequacy (one valid witness per mutant), and any claim of
the form “these mutants are certified killed”. The claims “this mutant is not killed”, “the semantic score
is exactly s” and “ϕ ≤ ϕ ′ semantically” are not checkable from witnesses alone. Every experimental
quantity in Section 9 is therefore a certified quantity at a stated adversary budget, and is named as such.
M-vacuity is relative to M, and classical vacuity implies it. If Pre is unsatisfiable no σ qualifies, and
if Post, Trace and Exit are tautologies no run violates ϕ; both give an empty kill set. The converse fails: a
specification with real content that no mutant in M violates is also M-vacuous. So M-vacuity does not
replace the separate checks that Pre is satisfiable (the Pre witness of v3, Sections 14.1 and 16) and that
the conclusion is non-trivial; it adds a library-relative check beside them, and the bundle keeps all three.

7 The specification game

The “game” is a sequential adversarial evaluation with a stated payoff; no equilibrium result is claimed,
and the word is kept only because the payoff is what the generator is paid.

Players. The generator G (untrusted) chooses the specification. The adversary A (untrusted) chooses
configurations for the declared mutants, that is, pairs in M × Σ; M itself is declared by the producer. The
kernel (trusted) evaluates. In the spec-only game studied here the artifact b and the natural-language
intent I are fixed; G’s job is to write ϕ for b. The two-sided game where G also chooses b is noted in
Section 11.

Moves. G moves first with a pair (ϕ, c). A moves second with a finite witness set W ⊆ M × Σ, produced
by a search procedure with a stated budget (fuzzing, symbolic execution, a model); A has no objective of
its own in this note. The kernel then evaluates. A’s search may be incomplete; Theorem 8.4 states the
consequence for the score.

8 Theorems

The four theorems are short. The first two describe incentives; the third carries the security content, and
its part (ii) is conditional on V -completeness; the fourth bounds what an incomplete adversary can do.

Theorem 8.1 (degenerate optimum of Accept-only reward). Under Assumptions TA and CS, (ϕ⊤, c⊤) is
an optimal strategy for G in Γ∅, and every optimal strategy has an empty semantic kill set.

Without these conditions maximizers need not be adequate: a hard game whose only adequate strategy
costs 1.2 loses to the trivial spec at payoff 0, and in a soft game a cheap one-kill spec at payoff 0.4 beats
a full-kill spec at 0.2. The stated conditions exclude both. One corner remains: under a steep κ the
soft optimum leaves expensive mutants alive. That is incompleteness, not gaming, and the experiment
separates the two by reporting kill sets, not scores.

Two readings follow. First, over a fixed, declared M with a kernel-checked numerator, an incomplete

9 From theorems to hypotheses

The theorems say what each reward makes optimal; the hypotheses say whether an LLM under iterative
feedback moves toward that optimum. The pilot is the first experiment: three contracts (a capped token,
a vault with withdrawal, an escrow), each with a hand-written library of fault-injected mutants, in the
prompt regime only; it tests H1 and H2. H3 and H4 need the training regime and form a second stage.

Feedback functions. Between rounds G observes f (ϕt, ct). Three conditions:

• f∅: the Accept bit only (plus compiler or checker errors).
• fM: the Accept bit and the certified score mscert
• fM+: as fM, plus the identity of surviving mutants.

payoff; here the score is a reward, and H3 predicts that the generator learns to exploit what the reward
does not see.

Hypothesis (H1: vacuity drift). Under f∅ the expected score is non-increasing in t, and weakening
events concentrate in rounds following a non-Accept. Motivation from Theorem 8.1: the cheapest repair
to a specification that fails on b is to weaken it. The FormalBench study [16] records a slight drop in
completeness as verification success rises; H1 asks whether that drop is a trajectory with a mechanism,
measured by kill sets rather than by an aggregate. Falsification criterion: no difference in weakening rate
between f∅ and fM at the pilot’s sample size, in either regime.

Hypothesis (H2: gate reversal; a replication). Under fM the expected score is non-decreasing in t and the
final rate of empty certified kill sets is lower than under f∅. AutoJML [18] reports the aggregate effect in

the prompt regime for Java/JML; per-round dynamics and a comparison with f∅ are not described there

Hypothesis (H3: overfitting to the declared library). Setup: a fault taxonomy is fixed first; two libraries
are then built from it independently, Mpublic and Mheldout, by separate procedures (a second person, or
an operator set fixed and hashed before training starts) through the same materialization pipeline, with
properness evidence for every element; Mheldout is never shown to G, never used for selection, and scored
with the same definition and the same BA. Prediction, training regime: the gap spublic − sheldout grows with
training steps under fM, and faster under fM+. The same gap under f∅ is the control. This is the corner
of Theorem 8.2 under pressure, and it is a question the work in Table 1 does not ask, because none of it
trains on the score.

Hypothesis (H4: false acceptance on held-out faults). The security-facing form of H3: among held-out
mutants, the fraction with no certified witness against the final ϕ at budget BA (a proxy for false acceptance
of a defective variant) is higher for generators trained under fM than for those under f∅ at matched spublic.
Three scores are kept apart throughout: spublic over Mpublic, sheldout over Mheldout, and sfull over their union;
none is a “true” score, and Theorem 8.4 is a statement about each one separately, never a comparison
between them.

Minimum protocol. Fixed before any run: the reward and κ (number of non-tautological conjuncts), R
and k for the prompt regime, the training recipe and checkpoint-selection rule for the training regime of
the second stage (selection on spublic only), seeds, models, BA, the three contracts and both libraries with
hashes. Primary metric: sheldout at the final round or checkpoint; secondary: weakening rate, final rate of
empty certified kill sets, spublic. Comparisons are paired across seed × contract with bootstrap intervals;
the pilot’s sample size is stated as a limit. Policies: a mutant that does not compile or deploy is excluded
from the library before the experiment; Check returning Unknown counts as non-Accept; a witness that
fails the kernel is discarded and logged, never counted.

What the pilot cannot establish. Generality beyond the three contracts; behavior under fine-tuning
(H3, H4); transfer to a Lean oracle.

11 Open questions

These are the places where the note is most likely wrong or thin; comments on them are the most useful.

1. Is ≤M the right coarsening? It treats every mutant as equal. A weighted order by vulnerability class,
or a partial order over a family of classes MV , would separate “strong against reentrancy, weak against
access control”. Does weighting buy anything the kill set as a set does not already show?

2. Can V -completeness be made checkable for concrete V ? For reentrancy expressed as a Trace
predicate, the set of fault injections that produce a reentrant call may be finite and enumerable for a
given b. If so, Theorem 8.3(ii) becomes unconditional for that V on that neighborhood. For which V
does this work?

3. What is κ for an LLM? The theorems assume Assumption CS: the trivial specification is cheapest,
strictly so against any specification with a non-empty kill set. Candidates: number of properties, length
of ϕ, number of rounds to Accept. The experiment can test which, if any, the generator responds to.

4. Does the soft game have a hidden degenerate optimum? Theorem 8.2 notes the corner where
expensive mutants are left alive. Is there a cost regime in which the soft optimum is strictly below
adequacy for every proper M, and does fM+ make it worse (H3)?

5. The two-sided game. When G also chooses b, M must be defined relative to the chosen b, and G can
choose a b for which M is accidentally improper. Is there a formulation where M is a function of the
intent I rather than of b?

10

6. Who audits the adversary? Theorem 8.4 moves gaming to the choice of M. A second gate on the
adversary (held-out mutants, as in H4) is one answer; is there a fixed point, or does every gate add a
layer?

7. Unkillable proper mutants. Properness is checked on full observable traces, but the specification
language may not express every observable (gas used, for example). A proper mutant that differs
from b only in such an observable is killed by no ϕ, so the score has a ceiling below 1 and adequacy
is unattainable. Should the bundle carry a ceiling estimate, and how would it be computed without
deciding which differences the language can express?

8. Transport to non-executable semantics. Without Condition E, witnesses are not ground and the gate
degrades to “the weakened spec is provable on the mutant”, which shows weakness but not strength.
Is there a weaker notion of strength that survives?

9. What is left once AutoJML is subtracted? The feedback loop and the completeness metric are
theirs. Is the game plus the training regime plus kernel-checked witnesses enough for a contribution,
or does the note need one more theorem with content (a checkable V -completeness for a concrete V ,
question 2) to stand on its own?

10. Duplicate and nondeterministic cases. Definition 4.1 counts behaviorally equal mutants with
different hashes twice, which inflates |M| and gives duplicated behaviors double weight in every score.
Is a hash-of-observable-trace-on-σ a usable dedup key, and what does the witness become in a domain
where run is set-valued?

11. Submission type. With H1–H4 unmeasured, this is a research note with an experimental program. A
working prototype of generation → mutant materialization → kernel witness check → recomputable
score is needed before the note can be called a research paper; until that exists it is submitted, if at all,
as a workshop or agenda paper.
